# Cabinet Luminance
*A design philosophy for the Science Brawl fighter sprites*

**Cabinet Luminance** is the discipline of making very small things feel monumental. It treats the
64-pixel figure the way a medieval illuminator treated a capital letter: a tiny field in which every
mark is deliberate, every color earns its place, and the whole reads instantly from across a room.
Nothing in this movement is generated casually; each sprite must look like it was pushed
pixel-by-pixel by a master of the CRT era — labored over, revised, and polished until the silhouette
alone names the character.

**Form and silhouette.** The figure is built from confident, chunky masses — a large expressive head
(one third of the body), a wedge of torso, limbs that taper. Identity lives in the outline: Einstein
must be recognizable filled with solid black. Poses are drawn at their extremes — the punch reaches,
the hit recoils, the block coils — because at this scale, gesture *is* animation. Every frame is a
held pose from a fight poster, meticulously balanced so weight always sits over the feet.

**Color and light.** Each character owns a strict, hand-tuned palette: three-step ramps (shadow,
body, light) for skin, garment, and hair, unified by a single deep violet-black ink that outlines
every silhouette and separates interior forms. Light falls from the upper front — a one-pixel
highlight crowns each mass, a one-pixel shade grounds it. Hue-shifted shadows (never mere darkening)
give the little figures the warmth of painted miniatures. Saturation is reserved for each
scientist's signature accent — the tie, the brooch, the glow of a discovery.

**Craft and rhythm.** The pixel grid is sacred: no half-tones, no smoothing, no accidental noise.
Detail is spent where the eye goes — eyes, brow, hands, hair — and withheld elsewhere so the figure
never becomes mush. Repetition builds the set into a system: twelve fighters share one anatomy, one
light, one ink, so together they read as a museum case of specimens, individually distinct,
collectively inevitable. Any pixel that cannot justify itself is removed.

**The reference beneath.** Each figure quietly encodes its owner's science — the geometry of an
orbit in a hair-bun, a helix in a scarf, a wreath of laurel — sensed by those who know, invisible
ornament to those who don't. The work should feel like an artifact from an alternate 1994: a
cartridge classic whose art team had endless time, taste, and reverence for the history of thought.
